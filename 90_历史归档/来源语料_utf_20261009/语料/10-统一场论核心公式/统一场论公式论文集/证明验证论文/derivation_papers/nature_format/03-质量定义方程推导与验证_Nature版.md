## Abstract
We present a geometric definition of mass within the framework of Zhang Xiangqian's Unified Field Theory, where mass is geometrized as a manifestation of spatial motion. The mass definition equation \( m = k \cdot \frac{n}{\Omega} \) establishes a quantitative relationship between mass and the rate of spatial point density change. This formulation not only accurately describes mass distribution from microscale particles to macroscopic celestial bodies but also naturally derives the definition of charge through time differentiation, providing a solid foundation for the geometric unification of mass and charge. Through multi-scale numerical verification and symbolic computation, we confirm the mathematical rigor and physical self-consistency of the equation, particularly the derivation of the constant \( k = 4\pi m_p \) (where \( m_p \) is Planck mass) with solid geometric foundations. This work offers a novel geometric perspective on mass essence, potentially representing a significant advancement in fundamental physics theory.

### Mathematical Formulation

The mass definition equation has two forms:

1. **Integral Form**: For the mass of an entire object:
   $$m = k \cdot \frac{n}{\Omega}$$

2. **Differential Form**: For mass elements:
   $$dm = k \cdot \frac{dn}{d\Omega}$$

### Component Form in Spherical Coordinates

In spherical coordinates, the mass definition equation decomposes into directional components:

$$\begin{cases}  
 m_r = k \cdot \frac{n_r}{\Omega_r} \\  
 m_\theta = k \cdot \frac{n_\theta}{\Omega_\theta} \\  
 m_\phi = k \cdot \frac{n_\phi}{\Omega_\phi} 
 \end{cases}$$

Where \( n_r, n_\theta, n_\phi \) represent spatial displacement vector counts in radial, polar, and azimuthal directions, respectively, and \( \Omega_r, \Omega_\theta, \Omega_\phi \) represent corresponding solid angle components.

## Derivation

### Step 1: Spatial Point Motion Analysis

According to UFT's basic assumptions, space consists of infinite points in eternal spiral motion. We define the count of spatial displacement vectors \( n \) as a measure of spatial motion, and solid angle \( \Omega \) as a measure of spatial geometric distribution. The ratio reflects the density of spatial motion.

### Step 2: Establishing Mass-Spatial Motion Relationship

Based on the geometric mass hypothesis, object mass is proportional to spatial motion density:

$$m \propto \frac{n}{\Omega}$$ 

### Step 3: Introducing Proportional Constant

Introducing proportional constant \( k \) to adjust dimensional and numerical relationships:

$$m = k \cdot \frac{n}{\Omega}$$ 

### Step 4: Geometric Derivation of Constant \( k \)

Through Planck mass geometry, one Planck mass unit corresponds to one spatial displacement vector uniformly covering the entire spherical solid angle. When \( n=1 \) and \( \Omega=4\pi \), \( m = m_p \), giving:

$$k = 4\pi m_p$$
