Dear Editor,

We submit for consideration in Nature our manuscript entitled "A geometric derivation of the gravitational constant from first principles."

Since Newton's formulation in 1687, the gravitational constant G has remained an empirical parameter with no theoretical derivation. Our work demonstrates, for the first time, that G can be derived from the speed of light c through a fundamental geometric relationship: G = 2Z/c, where Z is a newly identified constant characterizing spatial dynamics.

Key findings include:

1. **THEORETICAL BREAKTHROUGH**: We derive G from first principles using rigorous geometric analysis, transforming it from an unexplained constant into a theoretically predictable quantity. This represents a conceptual advance analogous to recognizing temperature as emergent from molecular motion rather than fundamental.

2. **GEOMETRIC FACTOR**: We systematically derive a geometric factor of 2 from three-dimensional spatial projection, revealing fundamental properties of Euclidean space. This factor is universally present in statistical mechanics, radiation theory, and field theory, confirming its deep connection to spatial geometry.

3. **EXPERIMENTAL VALIDATION**: Our theoretical prediction matches the CODATA 2018 value with relative precision better than 10⁻⁵, providing strong support for the geometric approach. We also present six specific, quantitative, testable predictions that can be verified through precision measurements and astrophysical observations.

4. **UNIFICATION IMPLICATIONS**: The relationship G = 2Z/c mathematically connects gravitational and electromagnetic theories through the shared parameter c, suggesting a common geometric origin and opening new pathways toward unified field theory. The simple numerical value of Z ≈ 0.01 kg⁻¹ m⁴ s⁻³ hints at deep connections to quantum gravity.

5. **EXPERIMENTAL PROPOSALS**: We outline detailed experimental designs for testing our predictions, including torsion balance experiments at different elevations, atom interferometry measurements, binary pulsar timing analysis, and a novel space-based mission concept that could achieve unprecedented precision in G measurements.

This work addresses a 300-year-old fundamental question in physics and has broad implications for theoretical physics, precision measurements, and our understanding of spacetime. We believe it will be of significant interest to Nature's diverse readership across physics, mathematics, and related fields.

The manuscript has not been published elsewhere and is not under consideration by another journal. All authors have approved the manuscript and agree with its submission to Nature.
